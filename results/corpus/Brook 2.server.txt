 Byron was at his best and most characteristic in the long poem, Chiled Harrell's pilgrimage was a sensation that made his name, and Don Juan was a book-linked virtuoso extravaganza that sealed the deal. But anyone who doubts that Byron could have shone if confined to shorter poems should take a close look at this one, and check out the perfection of his balance. One shade the more, one ray the less. It's just a tiny example of the impetus you could keep up for pages. The only thing that makes this particular bironic poem, non-bironic, is that it professedly to do with innocence. The innocence is at the heart of the narrator's fancy, the young lady, and certainly she is more innocent than Byron truly thought life, or love could ever be. She might come good later, i.e., she might turn bad, but at the moment she is just a stack of ice to cake, trembling in the strip slipstream as Byron sweeps by on his way to depravity's unknown in her sweet dreams.
